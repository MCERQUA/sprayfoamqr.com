Of all the decisions that went into how this directory works, the one we're least willing to compromise on is this: a contractor's actual insurance certificate — the PDF, the document itself — never appears on a public page. Not a download link, not an embedded viewer, nothing. It's worth explaining exactly why, because it's not an arbitrary rule.

## What's Actually on a Certificate of Insurance

A standard certificate of insurance (COI) typically includes the policy number, the named insured's exact legal business name and often their mailing address, the carrier and broker contact information, and coverage limits and dates. For a sole proprietor — which describes a meaningful share of spray foam contractors — that mailing address is frequently a home address, not a separate business location.

## Why Publishing That Is a Real Risk, Not a Theoretical One

A policy number in the wrong hands can be used for social engineering against the insurance carrier or the contractor directly. A home address published on a public, indexable web page is a real privacy exposure for a small business owner, especially one whose only "office" is a truck and a phone. None of this is hypothetical risk-modeling — it's the plain, predictable consequence of putting a raw insurance document on the open internet, searchable and permanent.

## What We Show Instead

Every profile shows a plain-language status line: something like "General Liability $2M — carrier on file." That tells a homeowner or general contractor the thing they actually need to know — is this business insured, and roughly how much coverage do they carry — without exposing the policy number, the carrier's internal reference, or a home address alongside it.

## What Happens If Someone Actually Needs the Real Document

A general contractor doing real due diligence before subcontracting work, or an insurance-conscious homeowner, sometimes legitimately needs the actual certificate — not just the summary. That's a direct request to the contractor, the same way it would be off this platform entirely. We're not trying to replace that relationship; we're trying to make the first 90% of the trust question — "are they even insured, roughly how much" — answerable in ten seconds without exposing sensitive data to everyone else who scans that same sticker.

## The Same Logic Applies to License Numbers and Home Addresses

This isn't only about insurance. A contractor's license number is generally public record through the state licensing board already, so we do show it — but we never show a home address, and we ask contractors during signup not to submit one as their listed service area. Service area is a city or region, never a street address.

## Why This Matters More as the Directory Grows

A single contractor deciding what to share is a personal choice. A directory that other contractors and homeowners increasingly rely on has a different obligation — the design choices here need to hold up at scale, not just for the first profile. Building the "never publish raw documents" rule into the platform itself, rather than trusting each contractor to redact their own submission, is what makes that hold.
